**Format:** 45 minutes, hiring manager, going deeper than round 1
**He named three areas:** customer marketing and advocacy · vendor and agency management · strategy and pace

## The read on their business

Pulled from public sources ahead of the call. Confirm anything you plan to say out loud.

- Pre-employment background and drug screening. Founded 1999, Phoenix AZ, 51 to 200 employees, privately held.
- Service lines: criminal records, driving records, credit, SSN trace, I-9 and E-Verify, employment verification, education verification, drug and alcohol collection (3,000+ facilities), continuous monitoring, social media screening, vendor screening, contingent labor support, student screening.
- Named verticals include staffing agencies.
- Their own positioning: PBSA accredited (they claim under 5% of providers are), staff hold individual FCRA certifications, US-based client care.
- LinkedIn shows "Frasco Profiles (An AccuSourceHR Company)", so there has been acquisition activity. Do not assert this as fact. Ask about it instead (see Questions).

**Why this shapes everything:** they sell a compliance purchase to a mature client base with
a long product ladder. Switching vendors is scary for the buyer, their differentiator
(accreditation, rigor, care) is invisible in a demo, and most clients almost certainly buy
one or two SKUs out of a dozen. That combination is why customer marketing and advocacy is
the first thing he listed, and it is the strategic thread to pull through all three areas.

## AREA 1 — Customer marketing and advocacy

**Your posture:** you have real expansion revenue experience and the closest thing to an
advocacy engine anyone builds at SMB scale. You have not built a B2B reference and case
study program. Say so, then show him you know exactly how you would.

### Story 1.1 — Expansion comes from diagnosis, not a menu

*Source: Solenzo. Google Ads optimization and retainer scope expansion.*

> At Solenzo I run marketing operations for service businesses on retainer. One client
> brought me in for Google Ads. Their spend was fine and their lead volume was fine, but
> the leads were junk. I cleaned up the account, cut the spam and low quality traffic, and
> click through quality improved. At that point the ads were doing their job and conversion
> was still soft, so I traced it past the ads to the site itself. The bottleneck had moved.
>
> That is the conversation that expanded the retainer. I was not pitching a CRO package. I
> was showing them where their money was still leaking after I fixed the thing they hired
> me for. The expansion came out of the diagnosis.

**Bridge:** "That is how I would think about cross-sell here. A client running criminal
records only is telling you something about their risk posture. If they are not also running
drug screening or continuous monitoring, that is a gap in their program, not a line item in
an upsell spreadsheet. The gap is the pitch, and it is a much easier conversation."

### Story 1.2 — The advocacy engine you actually built, and its ceiling

*Source: Solenzo. 20+ GoHighLevel workflows including automated review generation.*

> The closest thing I have built to an advocacy engine is at Solenzo. I built out a full
> GoHighLevel automation system, 20 plus workflows across the lifecycle, and one piece of it
> is automated review generation. It fires off a lifecycle event, so the ask reaches the
> customer at the moment they are most likely to say yes, instead of on a random cadence or
> whenever somebody remembers to ask.
>
> The thing that made it work is that advocacy dies when it is a task someone has to
> remember. Once it is triggered off the event, it runs whether anyone is paying attention
> or not.
>
> I will be straight with you about the ceiling on that. It is advocacy at volume for small
> businesses, reviews and testimonials. It is not a B2B reference program with named accounts
> and written case studies. The trigger logic transfers. The asset production does not. I
> would be building that part new.

**Why volunteer the limit:** he said "from close to scratch." He is going to probe this
anyway. Naming it first costs you nothing and buys you the credibility to spend the next two
minutes on the build plan, which is the part he actually wants to hear.

### Story 1.3 — You have sold customer voice as a product

*Source: Birdeye, Feb 2023 to Apr 2024. 15 to 20 reseller partners, ~94% retention, $10K to $45K deals.*

> I spent over a year at Birdeye on channel partnerships and onboarding. Birdeye is a
> reputation and customer voice platform, so the product I was selling and implementing was
> literally "turn your customers into your marketing." I onboarded and managed 15 to 20
> reseller partners, deals in the 10 to 45 thousand range across health, home services and
> retail, and I retained all but one.
>
> Here is what that taught me that matters for this role. Customer proof is a sales asset,
> not a marketing asset. The partners who got value out of it were not the ones with the
> prettiest testimonials page. They were the ones who put customer proof in front of a
> prospect at the exact moment of doubt. That changes how you build the program. You build
> it backwards from the sales objection it has to answer.

### The 90-day plan for their reference engine

This is the centerpiece of Area 1. Deliver it as a point of view, not a pitch.

**Days 1 to 30 — find the proof that already exists.** Nobody builds this from zero. Pull
tenure and retention data out of the CRM, find the accounts that have expanded service lines,
and go sit with client care. If their client care is genuinely award winning, that team
already knows every client who loves them. The raw material is in the CRM and in those
people's heads.

**Days 30 to 60 — put the ask in the lifecycle, not in a campaign.** Trigger the reference
request off a real moment: a clean implementation, a renewal, a compliance audit the client
passed. Start with three to five named accounts, weighted toward whichever verticals matter
most commercially. Produce short assets first. A one page quotable proof sheet that a rep
actually sends beats a six page case study nobody finishes.

**Days 60 to 90 — make it usable by sales and measure it.** A reference pool tagged by
vertical and by objection, with a rotation so the same three clients do not get burned out.
Then measure the only thing that matters: win rate and cycle length on deals where a
reference was used versus deals where one was not.

**The strategic line to close on:** "In a compliance purchase, a reference is not marketing
collateral, it is risk reduction. Nobody gets fired for choosing the screening vendor their
peer at a similar company vouched for. That is why this gets built properly instead of being
treated as a content project."

## AREA 2 — Vendor and agency management

**This is your strongest area. Lead with it if he gives you the choice.**

Almost nobody interviewing for this job has sat on the agency side. You have, twice. That is
the differentiator, and it answers his exact question ("how do you get the most out of a
vendor") from a place no other candidate can answer it from.

### Story 2.1 — You have been the vendor

*Source: Level Agency, client strategist on integrated inbound, paid media and content. Fetch & Funnel, ran accounts end to end.*

> I have been the vendor. At Level Agency I was the client strategist on integrated programs
> across inbound, paid media and content, and at Fetch and Funnel I ran client accounts end
> to end. So when you ask how I get the most out of a vendor, I am answering from having been
> on the receiving end of both good clients and bad ones.
>
> Two things I know from that side.
>
> Agencies triage quietly. The clients who get the senior people and the proactive thinking
> are the ones who are clear about what success looks like, who make decisions fast, and who
> give real feedback instead of going silent and then blowing up at the QBR. The clients who
> get the junior bench are the ones who treat the agency like a ticket queue.
>
> And I know where the padding lives. A retainer with vague deliverables gets filled with
> activity. A retainer tied to a specific outcome gets filled with work. I would rather spend
> the effort writing a tight scope up front than auditing hours on the back end.

**Tone warning:** this has to land as insight, not as grievance. You are not telling him
agencies are lazy. You are telling him you know how to be the client that gets their best
work. Say it warmly.

### Story 2.2 — You can grade the work

*Source: Solenzo. Technical SEO audits, keyword gap analysis, Google Ads cleanup, GA4 and CRM reporting.*

> The other half of it is that I can check the work, which changes the relationship. I run
> technical SEO audits and keyword gap analysis myself, I have gone into a Google Ads account
> and cut the spam and low quality leads out of it, and I build the CRM and reporting layer
> underneath all of it.
>
> So when an SEO vendor sends a report full of impressions and rankings, I know to ask what
> happened to qualified pipeline, and I can go look for myself instead of taking the report
> at face value. That is not adversarial. The strong vendors like it, because they finally
> have a client who can tell the difference between real work and volume.

**Bridge to their roster:** they have you directing paid media, rev ops and SEO partners.
You have hands-on depth in all three.

### Story 2.3 — How you would run the roster

Forward-looking, not a war story. Use this when he asks how you would operate.

- **One scorecard per vendor, tied to pipeline, not activity.** Impressions and deliverables count are inputs. The scorecard measures qualified pipeline and cost per qualified opportunity.
- **Monthly working session, quarterly business review.** The QBR is where you review direction. It is not where problems get discovered for the first time.
- **One internal owner per vendor.** You. Vendors should not be able to route around the person accountable for their results.
- **A 90 day proof window before any long commitment,** with the success criteria written down before the vendor starts.
- **You own the attribution layer, not the vendors.** This is the sharp one, and it comes straight out of your rev ops background: never let the vendor running paid media also be the sole source of truth on what paid media produced. Same dashboard, your numbers.

*Supporting note if it fits:* at Standing Akimbo you built vendor and supplier workflows from
the ground up in a heavily regulated business. It is old (2010 to 2012) so do not lead with
it, but it is real vendor ownership and the regulated environment rhymes with theirs.

## AREA 3 — Strategy and pace

### Story 3.1 — Building while it is running

*Source: Wix, Aug 2020 to Aug 2022. New vertical, proof of concept to profitable business line. Corroborated by Camille Ramirez's written recommendation.*

> At Wix I was on a brand new vertical that started as a proof of concept and turned into a
> profitable business line. I was onboarding and launching 25 plus clients a month, running
> at 120 percent of sales targets and 110 percent of implementation targets, and I led the
> team of onboarding specialists.
>
> The part that matters for a small team is that none of it was built yet. No playbook, no
> training, no process. I ended up helping hire and train new people and writing sales
> training for the tenured account executives, not because it was in my job description but
> because the vertical could not scale without it. My manager there wrote a recommendation
> about that, I am happy to send it over.
>
> That is the pace I am used to. You build the thing while it is already running.

### Story 3.2 — Sequencing is the skill

*Source: Accelo, Apr 2018 to Jul 2020. Top 3 of 8 to 15 reps while quota roughly doubled, ~$17,750/month services bookings.*

> At Accelo I sold and scoped CRM and PSA software to agencies and professional services
> firms. The part of that job I was best at was sequencing. When a client wants to change
> five systems, the answer is not a project plan for all five. It is ranking which one to
> change first so that the other four get easier. I built those sequenced implementation
> plans and handed them to the implementation team to execute against.
>
> I stayed top three in sales on a team of 8 to 15 for effectively my whole tenure while the
> quota roughly doubled, averaging around 17,750 a month in services bookings. The sequencing
> is a big part of why. You close more when the plan you are selling is one that can actually
> be delivered.

**Why this story:** "strategy and pace" is really a question about whether you know what to
do first. This is the cleanest evidence you have that you do.

### Story 3.3 — Your point of view on their revenue growth

He explicitly asked how you would think about driving revenue growth at AccuSourceHR. Have
this ready as a coherent answer, not an improvisation.

> You are twenty five years into this business, PBSA accredited, and your own materials lean
> hard on compliance rigor and US based client care. That is a business with real trust
> equity and a mature client base. So if I am thinking about revenue growth, I am not
> starting with net new logos. That is the expensive path and it is not where your advantage
> is.
>
> I would start inside the existing base, for three reasons.
>
> One, your product line is a natural ladder. Criminal, drug and alcohol, I-9 and E-Verify,
> education and employment verification, continuous monitoring, social media screening,
> vendor screening, contingent labor. My guess is most of your clients are on one or two of
> those. Every additional service line is revenue and stickiness at the same time.
>
> Two, your differentiator does not survive a demo. Accreditation and compliance rigor are
> hard to feel until something goes wrong. A peer at a similar company saying it out loud is
> what makes it real, which is exactly why the reference engine is worth building.
>
> Three, switching screening vendors is scary. That cuts both ways. It makes cold prospects
> expensive and it makes your existing clients more winnable than a stranger.
>
> The thing I would want to know in my first thirty days is what percentage of your base is
> single service line, and whether that is because they do not need the rest or because
> nobody ever asked.

That last line is the strongest thing you can say in this interview. It is a real question,
it shows you already think like the owner of the function, and it hands the conversation back
to him.

### Story 3.4 — Why a home, and why here

*Context: on the first call you already told him the recent role endings were layoffs, not
performance, and that you are looking for a home rather than another short stop. He knows
this. Do not re-explain it from scratch. Use it as a one-sentence callback, then land it on
AccuSourceHR specifically.*

**When to use it:** if he asks "why this role" or "why us," or if your work history comes up
again. Do not volunteer it unprompted just to get ahead of it — that plays as defensive. It
should land as a real answer to a real question, not as damage control.

> Like I mentioned last time, the recent moves were layoffs, not performance, and that's part
> of why I'm being deliberate about where I land next. I'm not looking for another short stop.
> I'm looking for a place I can build something that outlasts a single engagement.
>
> You're twenty five years into this business, privately held, and still growing, the Frasco
> acquisition tells me that. That's exactly the kind of company I'm looking for. Not because
> it's safe, but because the work I'd be doing here, the reference engine, the vendor roster,
> the cross-sell motion, is multi-year work. None of it pays off in a quarter. I want to be
> somewhere I can see it all the way through.

**Why this works:** it turns the one part of your history that could read as a weakness into
the most honest answer you'll give all interview. It also ties directly back to Story 3.3,
an established, mature company is the right place for someone who wants to build something
durable, not chase the next round of funding. Say it once, plainly, and move on. Do not
over-sell it or repeat "home" like a script.

## Questions to ask him

Pick three or four. These are designed to get you real information, not to perform interest.

1. What percentage of your client base buys more than one service line? And what is your read on why the single service line accounts stay that way?
2. When a prospect asks to talk to a reference today, what actually happens? Who handles it and how long does it take?
3. Of the three outside partners, paid media, rev ops and SEO, which one is producing and which one are you least sure about?
4. I saw Frasco Profiles listed as an AccuSourceHR company. How does that fit in? Is that a separate client base or is there cross-sell there that has not been worked yet?
5. Six months in, what has to be true for you to say this hire worked?

Question 3 is the one that gets you the most. His answer tells you whether you are being
hired to manage vendors or to replace one.

## The one thing to remember

He asked for specific programs, what they produced, and what you would do differently or the
same in his business. That third part is where most candidates fall apart, because they only
prepared the first two. Every story above ends with the bridge into AccuSourceHR. Those
bridges are the interview.
