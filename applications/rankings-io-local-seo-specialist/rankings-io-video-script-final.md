## 1. Local SEO campaign I'm most proud of

BTU Solar, a Colorado solar installer, is the campaign I'm most proud of. The problem
wasn't call volume, it was that junk calls and low-quality form fills were burying the
real demand, so nobody could tell what was actually working.

I started with the foundation instead of chasing more traffic: fixed the Google
Business Profile categories, services, and photos, cleaned up citations across
directories, built location pages around how people actually search for solar
installation, and put GTM and GA4 in so calls and forms could be measured against GBP
activity.

The campaign's still active, so I won't call it a finished case study. What I can say
for certain is the foundation is fixed and we can now tell a real inquiry from noise,
which gives the client an honest baseline instead of treating every call the same.

For a personal injury firm, it's the same discipline on a higher-stakes journey: Map
Pack visibility to an answered call to a qualified consultation to a signed case.
Different business, same principle, protect the intake path and report each stage
honestly.

## 2. Google Business Profile troubleshooting

My strongest Google Business Profile example isn't a suspension fix, it's an
attribution problem. At BTU Solar the profile was live, but we couldn't tell if local
search was actually producing a qualified consultation, and junk calls and weak form
fills were mixed into the same numbers. The site was also running paid traffic with no
GA4 on it.

I worked backward from the outcome that mattered: install GTM and GA4, define and test
the conversion events that counted, keep the traffic source attached to every
conversion, and check the website data against GBP calls, clicks, and direction
requests. That gave us a real baseline, tested numbers instead of raw platform metrics.

I haven't been the primary owner of a suspension or reinstatement, so I won't claim
that. My approach would be the same discipline: stop touching the profile, verify the
real-world business info against Google's guidelines, document the evidence, clear up
any duplicates, and file one focused escalation with a clean record. In legal
specifically, I'd be careful about the staffed-office requirement, the exact
practice-area category, and consistent firm info across legal directories, since those
are the things that actually trigger a suspension.

## 3. Explaining slower-than-expected results

I set the expectation early: the foundational fixes move fast, profile, citations,
pages, tracking, but stronger rankings and lead volume take longer because Google's
comparing you against competitors with years of reviews and authority already built.

If a client feels slow, I don't ask them to trust a timeline. I show the baseline,
what's been completed, and the leading indicators, split by stage: visibility
(rankings, impressions, profile views), inquiry (clicks, calls, direction requests,
form fills), and business outcome (qualified consultations, signed clients, revenue
where the CRM supports it).

For a law firm that split matters more than most. A Map Pack ranking isn't a signed
case, and a form fill isn't automatically a qualified lead.

If the leading indicators are moving, I say so and keep watching the next stage. If
they're flat, that's a diagnostic problem, not a waiting game, so I go through profile
relevance, category, review velocity, citation consistency, local content, technical
issues, the competitive gap, and the intake path. The client should leave knowing what
the data actually supports and what we're testing next.

## 4. Managing a heavy workload while maintaining quality

At Wix I onboarded and launched over 25 clients a month while holding 120% of my sales
target and 110% of my implementation target, running sales conversations, onboarding,
and launch coordination at the same time.

The way I handled that volume was making every account visible instead of relying on
memory or an inbox thread. Each client ran through the same checklist, deliverables,
due dates, dependencies, and a final review before handoff. I prioritized by what would
actually delay a launch or cost the client value, not by what came in first.

I run Solenzo the same way. I manage up to three client engagements while building the
agency's own automation systems, everything centralized in GoHighLevel and Notion. I
front-load the riskiest work early in the week and batch the recurring stuff into fixed
blocks.

And I don't let a blocker stay hidden. If something's blocked by a client, vendor, or
platform, I say so, name who owns it, and name the next action, instead of letting a
deadline slip without warning.

## 5. Staying current with Local SEO and AI

I follow Google Search Central, Search Engine Land, and the Local Search Forum, but I
don't let an article or a new tool change client work until I've tested it and seen
what the data actually shows.

The clearest example is the AI audit process I built at Solenzo, meant to cut the
manual time it takes to review a business's local SEO and conversion setup. The first
version was inconsistent enough that I wouldn't have put it in front of a client. So I
built a rubric, graded the output against it, and found exactly where the model was
reliable, where it wasn't, and where it needed a human to verify the facts.

AI didn't replace the judgment, it made the first pass faster. The final recommendation
still comes from verified facts and my own review.

For law firms I'd run that same standard on practice-area and city research, GBP
review, and competitor tracking. AI can speed up the repeatable part. It can't replace
local context, verified firm information, or a sound recommendation.
