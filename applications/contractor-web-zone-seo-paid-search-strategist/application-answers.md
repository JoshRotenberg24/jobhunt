# Contractor Web Zone — Application Question Answers

## Q1. Roofing contractor, 8 cities, one general page + several service pages, weak organic leads. How would you determine which services, cities, and pages to target?

**Status: ready to paste.**

I'd start in Search Console rather than guessing: pull impressions, clicks, and average position segmented by query and landing page, and look for two patterns. First, queries getting impressions but no clicks or poor position, which tells me where content exists but isn't competitive. Second, service or city combinations with real search volume that have no dedicated page at all, which is usually the bigger opportunity than fixing an existing page.

From there I'd build a simple matrix: eight cities down one side, the client's actual services across the top, and rank each cell by three things — local search volume, competitive difficulty from a quick SERP check, and business value to the client (which services they actually want more of, since not every service is equally profitable). I'd prioritize creating dedicated city-service pages for the highest-volume, lowest-competition, highest-value cells first, and only invest in expanding an existing generic page where the data shows real search interest that a broader page can't capture on its own. I wouldn't build all eight cities at once; I'd ship the top two or three, measure what happens in Search Console over a few weeks, and let that result decide where to go next.

## Q2. A roofing contractor has a $2,000/month Google Ads budget. How would you approach building a new lead-generation campaign from scratch?

**Status: ready to paste.**

At $2,000 a month I'd resist the temptation to cover every service and city at once — that budget spread thin produces mediocre data everywhere instead of a working signal anywhere. I'd start narrow: the one or two highest-intent, highest-margin services (typically roof replacement or storm/insurance-related repair rather than general maintenance), in the client's core one or two cities rather than all eight.

Structurally, I'd build tight ad groups around exact and phrase match for high-intent terms, with a strong negative-keyword list from day one to keep out DIY searches, job seekers, and unrelated "roofing supplies" traffic. Each ad group would point to its own landing page rather than a general homepage, with call tracking and form-conversion tracking validated before the campaign goes live, not after. I'd set a conservative daily budget for the first two weeks specifically to gather CTR, CPL, and early lead-quality data, then reallocate based on what's actually converting rather than what looks good on impressions. The first month's goal isn't scale, it's a clean enough signal to know where the next dollar should go.

## Q3. Organic traffic is stable, but website leads have dropped 40% over the last three months. What would you investigate first?

**Status: ready to paste.**

Before touching SEO or content, I'd check whether the leads actually dropped or whether the *measurement* of leads dropped, since that's the cheaper and more common answer. I'd verify GA4 and any call-tracking conversion events are still firing correctly, and check whether a tracking number, a form embed, a CMS update, or a tag manager change coincided with the drop. Silent tracking breakage is the single most common cause of a "leads fell but traffic didn't" pattern, and it's the fastest thing to rule out.

If tracking checks out, I'd look at the funnel itself rather than the top of it: form completion rate, page load time on mobile, and whether a recent site or CMS change altered a phone number, a form, or a call-to-action's visibility. I'd also check Google Business Profile and the local pack specifically, since a review-response lapse, a category or hours change, or new local competition can quietly redirect calls away from the client without touching organic search traffic at all. Only after ruling out tracking and conversion-path issues would I look at whether the traffic mix itself shifted toward lower-intent queries, which stable overall traffic can mask.

## Q4. Describe one SEO or paid-search account you personally worked on. What was the problem when you took it over, what did you personally change, what tools or data did you use, and what happened as a result?

**Status: ready to paste, with one honest gap flagged below.**

At Solenzo, I run a Google Ads account for one of my own service-business clients. When I took a closer look at it, the click and impression numbers looked fine, but the leads coming through weren't — a real share of form submissions were spam, low-intent, or clearly outside the service area, which is invisible if you're only watching CTR and cost-per-click.

I rebuilt the targeting and expanded the negative-keyword list to filter out the categories generating that noise, tightened geo-targeting to the client's actual service area, and reviewed the ad copy against what was actually converting versus what was just getting clicks. Click-through quality improved and the spam volume dropped noticeably. But I didn't stop there: once the lead quality improved, the conversion rate on the site itself still wasn't where it should have been, so I traced that gap to the website's conversion path rather than calling the ad account fixed. The real lesson from that account is the one your posting names directly: cost per lead alone would have told me the campaign was working before it actually was.

*One honest note: I don't have an exact before/after CPL or lead-volume percentage documented for this account. If your process asks for a specific number in an interview, I'll be direct that the improvement was real and qualitatively significant (a visibly cleaner lead flow and higher click quality) rather than invent a precise figure I don't have on record.*
