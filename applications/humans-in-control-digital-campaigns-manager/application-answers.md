## 6. One argument for AI rules that lands with conservatives but not progressives, and vice versa

**Lands with conservatives, falls flat with progressives:** "No company gets to write its own rules. A family business has to follow the law; a handful of tech billionaires shouldn't get a pass. And parents, not a chatbot company, should decide what their kids are exposed to." Accountability for the powerful and parental authority land. With progressives, "parental rights" reads as culture-war code.

**Lands with progressives, falls flat with conservatives:** "AI is being sold to employers as a cheaper way to replace workers, and the gains go to a few companies while working people carry the cost. We need protections before the layoffs, not after." Worker protection and concentrated corporate power land. Conservatives hear class conflict and a call for more federal bureaucracy.

**The craft point:** both audiences agree nobody should grade their own homework. Lead with that shared frame, and use the audience-specific proof (a deepfake of a local teenager, a data center the town voted against) as the example, not the argument.

## 7. The most serious risks from advanced AI

What worries me most comes from building with these tools. Agentic systems take actions, not just write text, and they produce confident, wrong output often enough that I grade every result against a rubric before it reaches a client. Scale that up to systems with more autonomy and access, built by companies that admit they can't fully explain them, and loss of control stops sounding hypothetical. I'm also worried about power pooling in a few companies that set their own rules, and about harms already here, like deepfakes of real people.

What feels overblown is the idea that one dramatic moment will announce the danger. The more likely path is gradual: more decisions handed to systems nobody fully understands, one reasonable-looking step at a time. That's why rules have to come before the crisis, not after it.
